## Presenter Background and Session Overview
The speaker shares their experience from a startup using AI to detect abnormalities in lung CT scans, which was acquired by a larger company. They now run a consultancy helping with medical device compliance and participate in standardization committees like CEN/TC 251 and IEC/TC 62. The session covers AI Act basics, its interplay with medical device regulations (MDR/IVDR), risk management, quality systems, technical docs, and conformity assessment, with a break planned midway.

## AI Act as Horizontal Legislation and Interaction with MDR
The AI Act sets broad requirements for AI systems and general-purpose AI, while also regulating specific sectors like medical devices via Annex I. It overlaps with MDR for products requiring conformity assessment, but creates complexity as a self-standing law too. High-risk AI systems under the Act include Class IIa, IIb, and III medical devices, but Class I (unless sterile/measuring/invasive) are exempt. In-house hospital AI gets a waiver, with more guidance coming.
* **Summary**: AI Act is horizontal, applying across laws like MDR, but also vertical for unregulated AI like education systems. Recent MDCG guidance clarifies MDR interactions.
* **Key Takeaway/Example**: A CT scanner with retrospective AI quality check might not qualify as high-risk if it doesn't affect patient safety directly—needs clarification.

## Key Roles: Provider and Deployer
Providers (like manufacturers) place AI on the market and bear most responsibilities. Deployers (e.g., hospitals) install and use it under their authority. A doctor structurally using tools like ChatGPT with patient data might qualify as a deployer, implying obligations—guidance needed.
* **Summary**: These roles mirror MDR but add nuances, like excluding non-professional use.
* [Ask AI: AI Provider and Deployer Roles](https://alisol.ir/?ai=AI%20Provider%20and%20Deployer%20Roles%7CEIS%7CEU%20AI%20Act%3A%20Current%20Status%20and%20Developments)

## Risk Management Requirements
Risk management spans the full lifecycle, prioritizing design measures, alerts, and info for safety—similar to MDR. Differences include assessing fundamental rights risks (e.g., privacy, non-discrimination) and testing multiple mitigation options for effectiveness, possibly in real-world conditions (but tricky for medical devices).
* **Summary**: Specific risks called out: automation bias (over-reliance on AI), racial/ethnic bias, vulnerable groups (e.g., under 18), data processing issues, and feedback loops in continuous learning systems.
* **Key Takeaway/Example**: For skin cancer apps, consider skin color variations to avoid bias; standards like BS/AAMI 34971 (AI-specific annex to ISO 14971) help identify medical AI risks.
* [Ask AI: AI Risk Management](https://alisol.ir/?ai=AI%20Risk%20Management%7CEIS%7CEU%20AI%20Act%3A%20Current%20Status%20and%20Developments)

## Navigating Risk Management Standards
Multiple overlapping standards create complexity: ISO 14971 for medical devices, BS/AAMI 34971 for AI in medical devices, CEN/TC 251's upcoming EU standard, and ISO/IEC 23894 globally. Timelines are tight—drafts like CEN's risk management expected end-2026/early-2027, but compliance required by Aug 2027.
* **Summary**: Expect a "maze" of standards; medical notified bodies likely prioritize 14971/34971, while AI Act harmonized standards address fundamental rights.
* **Key Takeaway/Example**: CEN/TC 251 draft got 350 pages of comments on 70-page doc—process is messy and slow.

## Quality Management System (QMS) Updates
QMS must include regulatory compliance strategy, similar to MDR (combinable with ISO 13485). Additions: procedures for substantial modifications, predetermined changes (pre-approve future updates like FDA), and AI-specific standards like updated IEC 62304 (AI annex), IEC 62366-1 (usability for AI), and upcoming CEN/TC 251 QMS standard.
* **Summary**: Cover design/development, quality control, testing (e.g., A/B testing via IEC 63450), data governance (acquisition, labeling, storage via ISO 5259 series), post-market monitoring (plan due Feb 2026), vigilance (align with MDR but report fundamental rights infringements to AI authority), and resource management (e.g., data suppliers).
* **Key Takeaway/Example**: For vigilance, Estonia's consumer protection/technical regulator handles AI Act; medical incidents go to health board.
* [Ask AI: AI Quality Management Systems](https://alisol.ir/?ai=AI%20Quality%20Management%20Systems%7CEIS%7CEU%20AI%20Act%3A%20Current%20Status%20and%20Developments)

## Data Access and Record Keeping in QMS
QMS must enable data access for notified bodies, operators, etc.—implications for agreements with data providers. Keep automated logs (e.g., human oversight actions) for 6 months; include cybersecurity (upcoming standard).
* **Summary**: SMEs can simplify QMS, but unclear how it interacts with MDR. Monitor standards via IEC/TC 62 WG SNAKE reports and CEN/TC 251 work program.
* **Key Takeaway/Example**: If data sets aren't in-house, ensure contracts allow sharing with notified bodies, including past versions—risk of market withdrawal otherwise.

## Technical Documentation Requirements
Combine with MDR docs, but add AI specifics: general description (versioning, hardware, UI), detailed AI description (pre-trained models, logic/algorithms, architecture, resources). Data management plan covers lifecycle, specs (e.g., age/gender stats), risks (bias, skewness), and subgroup performance.
* **Summary**: Standards delayed (e.g., to 2027+), but use NBMed AI questionnaire for guidance. Test generalizability across subgroups (e.g., FDA-style breakdowns).
* **Key Takeaway/Example**: Use tools like Matrix Requirements for tracing requirements/risks/tests—avoids unmanageable Word/Excel setups.

## Human Oversight, Testing, and Cybersecurity
Document oversight measures in risk assessment/IFU (e.g., trend analysis, not per-result review). Include "stop button" for safe halt. Test accuracy/robustness, discriminatory impacts; address cyber risks like data poisoning.
* **Summary**: Post-market: monitor accuracy on subgroups. Declaration of conformity must claim GDPR compliance—may trigger extra scrutiny.
