## Deciding to Negotiate: Overcoming the Barriers

**Summary**: Chris Croft starts by explaining why negotiation is crucial in both work and personal life—it can dramatically boost profits or save significant money with minimal effort. The biggest obstacle isn't skill but simply deciding to try. Many avoid it due to excuses like lack of time, fear of embarrassment, pride, or losing the deal. These are mostly false, and practice builds confidence.  

**Example**: Instead of reluctantly saying "yes" to an unwanted weekend visit with in-laws or a tough training gig, negotiate details like timing, bringing your laptop, or quoting a higher fee. This creates a win-win middle ground rather than a flat yes or no.

## Why We Avoid Negotiating and How to Push Past It

**Summary**: Common fears include embarrassment, looking cheap, fear of rejection, or damaging relationships. Chris encourages viewing negotiation as a game where both sides expect it. Overcome pride by reframing situations and using mental tricks like assuming the seller is laughing at the full price.  

**Example**: In a watch shop story, Chris faced awkward manager glances while haggling but treated it as a game, securing £8 off. The discomfort was worth it for the practice, as future deals (like houses) could save far more.

## Fear of Losing the Deal and the Power of Crumbling

**Summary**: You can't lose a deal just by negotiating politely. Walk away only if prepared to return (crumble). Blame affordability on yourself to stay friendly. Realizing you can always come back removes the fear.  

**Example**: On honeymoon in Greece, Chris walked away from sandals but had to return in "disguise" the next day. Lesson: Crumble immediately if needed rather than delay—pride isn't hurt because it's just a game, and you still get the item.

## Planning Your Negotiation: The Foundation of Success

**Summary**: Planning is critical. Set a firm internal walkaway point (never break it) based on personal value, not market rates. Distinguish internal factors (your walkaway) from external (opening offer based on market). Prepare tradables (extras with assigned values) and speculate on the other side's weaknesses to build strength.  

**Example**: For a dream holiday at £8,000 with a £5,000 walkaway, Chris would walk away even at £5,020 to reinforce his limits. In the circus ticket story, he walked away over £10 with his family, proving resolve for future negotiations.

## Making Strong Opening Offers

**Summary**: Don't open first if possible—ask questions to let them reveal info and weaknesses. Set an ambitious (non-round) opening offer lower/higher than the best you might achieve, justified appropriately. Use the "flinch" reaction to show shock and pressure movement.  

**Example**: Buying a pine table, Chris opened ambitiously low at £12 (felt outrageous at the time) against a £100 ask, eventually getting it for £28. The story shows ambitious opens work and look reasonable in hindsight.

## Key Negotiation Tactics to Know

**Summary**: Master tactics like the Vice (keep saying "do better"), Salami (watch for small incremental asks), Knocking the Product (counter by saying it's reflected in price), and Reluctant Buyer/Seller. Stay friendly, trade rather than concede unilaterally.  

**Example**: Against the Vice, ask "how much better exactly?" to force specifics. For salami-slicing, trade back or retract previous concessions if needed.

## The Art of Trading for Win-Win Outcomes

**Summary**: Use "If you... then I..." phrasing for trades. Move in small steps to signal you're near your limit while gaining concessions. Focus on tradables for true win-win (beyond pure price).  

**Example**: The hedge trimming story—seller jumped from £40 to £30 too quickly and lost the deal by not trading or persisting. Proper small-step trading with extras (e.g., tea, clippings removal) would have secured value.

## Closing the Deal Carefully

**Summary**: Avoid "final offer" phrases that weaken future positions. Handle "split the difference" by holding firm or splitting the split. Watch for the Nibble (post-agreement extras) and Quivering Pen (pre-signature asks)—counter or anticipate them. Walk away only if beyond your limit; otherwise trade or crumble.  

**Example**: Sofa feet "nibble" cost extra £40 unexpectedly. Counter-nibble on a training location in Leeds by charging mileage. Always ask about extras mid-negotiation.

## Practicing and Building Your Skills

**Summary**: Practice immediately in low-stakes settings like car boot sales, unwanted items, or things you can't afford. Repeat the course, try techniques, and refine. Negotiation saves thousands once mastered.  

**Example**: Negotiate at bike shops for practice or when spotting cheaper alternatives elsewhere—without revealing the competitor to potentially beat their price.
